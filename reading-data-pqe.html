Water in the World · Lessons 2.1–2.2 · Map & Data skills

Reading data like a geographer

One skill, three levels. First watch it done (the water example), then do it yourself (the rainfall map).

The move: P · Q · E

Any time you describe a graph, chart or map, do these three things — in this order.

P — Pattern
The general trend. What's the overall story? Use direction words (rises, falls, from… to…).
Q — Quantify
Prove it with numbers. Quote 2–3 real figures, with the units and the place.
E — Exception
The odd one out, or the surprise the pattern hides. This is the bit that gets top marks.
Watch it done — “I do / we do”

How much fresh water does each person get?

Renewable fresh water available per person, per year (cubic metres), in five countries. The red lines are the world “water stress”, “scarcity” and “absolute scarcity” levels.

Approximate, rounded values — total renewable freshwater per capita (World Bank / FAO AQUASTAT). Thresholds: Falkenmark water-stress <1,700, scarcity <1,000, absolute scarcity <500 m³/person/yr.

👉 Cover the answers. Ask the class to try each level first, then reveal.

Grade 5–6Continuum 2.1 · Level 6 — identify▾
“I can identify the key features of a graph.” Find the title, the units, the biggest, the smallest, and read a value.
The graph shows renewable fresh water per person in five countries, in cubic metres per year. Canada has the most, about 80,000 m³ per person. Jordan has the least, about 120 m³ per person. Egypt and Jordan are both in the red band, so they are water-stressed.
Grade 7–8Continuum 2.1 · Level 7–8 — describe with PQE▾
“I can describe patterns, quantify them, and point out exceptions.” One PQE paragraph.

PFresh water per person is spread very unevenly — it drops steeply from water-rich countries like Canada and Australia to water-scarce ones like Egypt and Jordan.

QCanada has about 80,000 m³ per person a year, while Jordan has only about 120 — more than 600 times less. Egypt (~600) and Jordan (~120) both sit far below the water-scarcity line of 1,000 m³.

EThe surprise is that Australia sits near the top (~21,000 m³) yet still builds desalination plants — so a national average can hide where the water actually is.

Grade 9–10Continuum 2.1 · Level 9–10 — explain & analyse▾
“I can use SHEEPT factors to explain trends” and “analyse relationships between sources.” Explain why, then judge the data.

Explain (SHEEPT): The pattern is mostly environmental — Canada's cool, wet climate and huge rivers give enormous runoff, while Egypt and Jordan sit in hot deserts where little rain falls. There is a political-geography twist too: much of Egypt's water is the Nile flowing in from other countries, so its “renewable” water is not fully its own.

Analyse / evaluate: The per-person average is misleading in two ways. It hides distribution within a country — Australia's water is in the empty north, not the crowded south, which is exactly why it desalinates — and it can count water that depends on a neighbour's rainfall. So “renewable” does not always mean available, or reliable.

Notice the Grade 7–8 exception and the Grade 9–10 point are the same idea: Australia's water is unevenly spread. That's the exact thing you'll map next. 👇

Now you do it — “you do”

Where does Australia's rain fall?

Australia looked water-rich in the last graph — but the water isn't spread evenly. Use P·Q·E to describe how rainfall is distributed across the country.

very wet (>2000 mm) wet moderate dry arid (<300 mm)
Schematic — drawn to teach the pattern, not to scale. For the real activity, project the live Bureau of Meteorology average rainfall map (free, CC-BY).

Your task

  1. Read the map. Find the wettest areas and the driest area. Read the rough rainfall numbers off the legend.
  2. Write a P·Q·E paragraph describing how rainfall is distributed across Australia. Use the words distribution and precipitation.
  3. Explain it (stretch). Why is it wet where it's wet? Think about the coast, mountains and the hot dry centre.

How good is your answer? (aim for your level, then push one higher)

Grade 5–6 · identifyI found the wettest and driest areas and read a rainfall figure for each. I used compass directions (north, south-east…).
Grade 7–8 · describe (PQE)My paragraph has a clear Pattern, is Quantified with 2–3 real figures and places, and names one Exception or surprise.
Grade 9–10 · explainI explained why using factors (oceans, mountains/rain shadow, latitude, the summer monsoon), and I noted what a single map can't tell you.
